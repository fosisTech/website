In the autumn of 1963, a small group of Muslim students at British universities did something quietly revolutionary. They decided that their individual voices were not enough. That the issues they faced, prayer spaces, halal food, discrimination, the particular loneliness of being a religious minority in a secular institution, required collective action. They founded FOSIS.

Sixty-one years later, the case for a national Muslim student voice is not just as strong. It is stronger.

## A Shifting Landscape

The environment for Muslim students in British higher education has changed significantly over the past decade, and not always for the better. Counter-extremism legislation has, in the view of many Muslim students and academics, created a chilling effect on legitimate political and religious expression on campus. Several universities have imposed restrictions on speakers, events, and student publications in ways that have disproportionately affected Muslim students’ unions.

At the same time, the cost-of-living crisis has hit Muslim students particularly hard, many of whom come from lower-income households, are the first in their family to attend university, and face compounded financial pressure. The withdrawal of maintenance grants, the rise in tuition fees, and the acute housing crisis in many university cities have made the economics of higher education increasingly punishing for first-generation students.

## What Representation Actually Means

FOSIS does not exist to be a grievance machine. We are not interested in performing victimhood or in reducing the extraordinary complexity of Muslim student life to a list of complaints. What we are interested in is power: the power to shape the conditions in which 350,000 Muslim students study, pray, eat, socialise, and become themselves.

That means sitting at tables where decisions about higher education policy are made, and making sure Muslim student perspectives are not an afterthought. It means training the next generation of Muslim leaders who will, in turn, occupy those tables themselves. And it means building a community strong enough that when the political climate shifts, as it always does, Muslim students have the institutional infrastructure to weather it.

## The Case for Affiliation

Every ISoc that affiliates with FOSIS strengthens this project. Affiliation is not a bureaucratic formality, it is an act of solidarity. It says: we are part of something larger than our campus, our city, our region. Our struggles and our successes belong to a national community, and we take responsibility for that community in return.

There are currently around 90 ISocs affiliated with FOSIS. There are over 200 Islamic Societies at UK and Irish universities. That gap represents 110 communities of Muslim students who are navigating university life without the backing of the national network. We want to change that.
