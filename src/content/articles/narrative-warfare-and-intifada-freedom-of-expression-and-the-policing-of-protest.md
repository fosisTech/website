On **17 December**, [two people were arrested](https://apnews.com/article/hate-speech-london-manchester-antisemitism-bondi-29dbba8d0abafa2ac52591191d66ba49) in central London for *racially aggravated public order offences* after allegedly chanting slogans that included calls for “intifada” at a pro-Palestinian protest. These arrests followed earlier statements from the Metropolitan Police and Greater Manchester Police indicating that individuals chanting or displaying placards with the phrase *“globalise the intifada”* could face arrest.

The term *intifada*, an Arabic word meaning *uprising* or *revolution*, has long held political and historical significance. The decision to treat its use as inherently criminal raises serious concerns about **freedom of expression**, **selective policing**, and the expanding scope of state censorship. When language associated with resistance or political struggle is weaponised and framed as inherently harmful, it compels us to reflect on the broader implications for civil liberties and democratic society.

The first intifada against Israel was a result of Palestinians living under brutal Israeli occupation, illegal settlement expansion and apartheid[1]. A frustration, which by 1987, had been growing for 20 years. At the time, illegal settlements in the occupied West Bank and Gaza Strip had been spreading, while Palestinians struggled economically and endured frequent confrontations with the Israeli army. At the same time the Israeli crackdown was being internationally condemned for being excessively violent. **Palestinians threw rocks. Israelis fired bullets**. Israeli soldiers deliberately beat protesters to break bones. They were also accused of torture. The UN Security Council would go on to condemn the Israeli government’s actions.

![Palestinians seek safety as armed Israeli soldiers fire at them during the first Intifada in the West Bank in January 1988 (Cal3b Gee/Pinterest)](/images/articles/inline/1009237e_920x610.jpg)

Following the recent mass shooting at Bondi Beach in Sydney, authorities stated: *“Violent acts have taken place, the context has changed, words have meaning and consequence. We will act decisively and make arrests.”* In the aftermath, political actors and organisations, including the Community Security Trust[2], were quick to draw tenuous links between this violence and pro-Palestinian activism. Such narratives risk conflating political solidarity with extremism, further narrowing the space for legitimate dissent.

At the same time, the Israeli state continues to advance a narrative that positions itself as the primary victim, using these moments to deflect scrutiny and rehabilitate its international image[3], the rhetoric of Muslim v Jew is pushed despite the person who stepped in to stop the attacker being a Muslim. This approach diverts attention from the root causes of ongoing violence and injustice. History shows that when structural causes are ignored, cycles of violence are not resolved, they are reinforced.

As students and as members of wider society, it is vital that we remain alert to these developments and committed to safeguarding the right to protest, speak, and organise without fear. Calls for justice must always remain rooted in lawful resistance.

## **Action Points for Muslim Students**

- **Know your rights** Familiarise yourself with your legal rights around protest, freedom of expression, and stop-and-search powers. Student unions and civil liberties organisations often provide clear guidance. [Find more here](http://greenandblackcross.org/action/know-your-rights)

- **Engage safely and lawfully** If attending demonstrations or campus events, prioritise personal safety, act within the law, and attend with trusted peers.

- **Look after your wellbeing** The current climate can be emotionally draining. Make use of university wellbeing services, chaplaincy support, or trusted community spaces when needed.

- **Challenge misinformation thoughtfully** Where appropriate, respond to inaccurate or harmful narratives with facts, context, and calm discussion, particularly in academic or student settings.

- **Build solidarity on campus** Work with student societies, faith groups, and allies to create spaces for dialogue, education, and mutual support.

- **Document concerns responsibly** If you experience or witness unfair treatment, intimidation, or discrimination/Islamophobia, keep records and report through appropriate university or legal channels.

- **Stay informed** Seek out a range of credible sources, including academic, legal, and human rights perspectives, to better understand the broader context.

- **Reach out to FOSIS** Use our [reporting tool](http://fosis.uk/report) if you or your ISoc face any danger, Islamophobia, discrimination, issues with Prevent, activism-related disciplinary cases or the like.
