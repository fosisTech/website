# How to Build a Thriving ISoc: 10 Lessons from the UK’s Best Islamic Societies

After six decades supporting ISocs across the UK, we know what separates a struggling society from a transformative one. Here are the ten things the best ISocs do differently.

Every September, thousands of new students arrive at university and walk into an Islamic Society for the first time. For some, it becomes the most formative experience of their degree. For others, it is a disappointment they quietly move past. The difference between those two experiences rarely comes down to resources or location. It comes down to leadership.

FOSIS has been working with ISocs for over sixty years. We have seen them rise and fall, thrive and struggle, transform communities and miss their moment. Over that time, we have identified ten things that consistently separate the great ones from the rest.

## 1. They prioritise belonging before programming

The best ISocs spend their first few weeks of term focused entirely on making new students feel welcome, seen, and wanted. Not on advertising events. Not on recruitment drives. On belonging. Everything else flows from that.

## 2. They have a clear identity, and stay true to it

Trying to be everything to every Muslim student is a recipe for mediocrity. The strongest ISocs know exactly what they stand for, what kind of community they are building, and what they are not. That clarity is magnetic.

## 3. Their committee is a team, not a hierarchy

The ISocs that burn out fastest are the ones where everything runs through one or two people. The ones that last are those where responsibility is genuinely distributed, where every committee member feels ownership, and where leadership is developed at every level.

## 4. They plan transitions, not just events

One of the most underrated skills in ISoc leadership is institutional memory. The best societies treat committee handover as a formal process, documented, deliberate, and adequately resourced. The weakest ones start from scratch every year.

## 5. They engage their university, not just their community

The most impactful ISocs are not just serving Muslim students, they are shaping the culture of their university. They sit on students’ union committees. They run interfaith events. They respond to campus controversies. They are visible in the broadest sense of the word.

## 6. They are honest about what isn’t working

The ISocs that stagnate are the ones that keep running the same events to the same declining attendance, too proud or too tired to ask why. The ones that grow are the ones that regularly evaluate, listen to members, and change what isn’t serving the community.

## 7. They invest in spiritual development, not just social programming

An ISoc that only runs socials is a social club. The strongest societies create genuine spaces for Islamic learning, reflection, and growth, and they do so in ways that feel relevant and alive to their members’ actual lives.

## 8. They look after their leaders

Committee burnout is endemic in ISoc culture. The best societies build rest, peer support, and genuine pastoral care into how they operate. You cannot pour from an empty vessel, and a burned-out committee produces a hollow community.

## 9. They stay connected to the national picture

ISocs that affiliate with FOSIS consistently outperform those that don’t, because affiliation means access to training, to resources, to a national network of peers who are facing the same challenges and finding solutions. No ISoc should have to reinvent the wheel.

## 10. They measure success differently

The best ISocs are not chasing footfall. They are asking: did this student leave university with a stronger sense of identity? Did they find community here when they needed it? Did their faith deepen? Are they contributing to something good in the world? Those are the metrics that matter.

If your ISoc is struggling, or if you want to take it to the next level, FOSIS is here to help. Explore our [ISoc Essentials](https://fosis.org.uk/isoc-essentials/) resources, or reach out to your regional chair directly.
