# Islamophobia on Campus: What the Data Tells Us, and What We’re Doing About It

FOSIS’s latest research into the experiences of Muslim students reveals a campus environment that is improving in some ways, and deteriorating in others. Here is what we found.

Every two years, FOSIS surveys Muslim students across the UK and Ireland to understand their experiences on campus. The 2024 findings, drawn from responses by over 4,200 students at 87 universities, paint a picture that is complex, concerning, and, in certain areas, quietly hopeful.

## The Headline Numbers

Fifty-four percent of Muslim students surveyed said they had personally experienced or witnessed an Islamophobic incident on campus in the past 12 months. Of those, just 31% reported it through a formal university channel. The most common reasons given for not reporting: “I didn’t think it would change anything” (58%), “I wasn’t sure it counted as Islamophobia” (34%), and “I was worried about being seen as difficult” (29%).

These figures are broadly consistent with national research from the Muslim Council of Britain and academic institutions. But there are local variations that matter. Students at post-1992 universities reported significantly higher rates of overt hostility than those at Russell Group institutions, where incidents tended to be more subtle and structural in nature.

## The Forms Islamophobia Takes

Islamophobia on campus is not a single thing. Our research identified several distinct patterns: overt verbal abuse (more common in student accommodation and social settings), microaggressions in academic and professional settings, institutional barriers such as the lack of halal food or prayer spaces, and what we term ‘administrative Islamophobia’, the way bureaucratic processes can inadvertently disadvantage Muslim students.

One pattern that has grown significantly since our last survey is what students describe as ‘political exclusion’, the experience of having their views on global events treated as inherently suspect or presumed to be extremist. In an open-ended question, 23% of students cited this as the form of Islamophobia they found most difficult to navigate. “I can handle someone being rude to me,” wrote one student. “What I can’t handle is being treated as a potential threat every time I open my mouth in a seminar.”

## What Institutions Are Getting Right

There is also progress to report. The number of universities with a dedicated Muslim student liaison officer has increased from 18 to 31 since 2022. Halal food availability on campus has improved significantly, with 76% of students now rating it as ‘good’ or ‘excellent’, up from 58% two years ago. And 89% of students said their ISoc had a positive or very positive impact on their sense of belonging on campus, the highest figure we have ever recorded.

## FOSIS’s Response: Islamophobia Training

One of FOSIS’s most impactful responses to these findings has been our Islamophobia Awareness Training programme, delivered to university staff, students’ union officers, and student welfare teams. Since 2019, we have trained over 2,000 individuals across 45 institutions.

The training is not simply a session on what Islamophobia is. It covers the history of anti-Muslim prejudice in Britain, how to recognise its institutional forms, how to respond to incidents as a bystander, and how to build environments where Muslim students feel genuinely safe. Participants consistently rate it as the most impactful diversity training they have received.
