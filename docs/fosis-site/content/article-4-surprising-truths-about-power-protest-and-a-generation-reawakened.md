# 4 Surprising Truths About Power, Protest, and a Generation Reawakened

By alibudhwani · Reading time loading…

`[marquee ticker]` FOSIS Articles · Est. 1963 · Muslim Student Voices · UK & Ireland

![](https://i0.wp.com/fosis.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/FOSIS-400x300-1.webp?fit=400%2C300&ssl=1) <!--400x300-->

It’s a common and logical assumption: as minority communities gain more visibility and representation in mainstream society, acceptance grows, prejudice fades, and progress naturally follows. The more members of a community we see in politics, sports, and media, the better their collective situation becomes.

But the reality on the ground is often far more complex and counter-intuitive.

Here are **four of the most surprising and impactful truths** from Dilly Hussain’s address, offering a new lens through which to view the relationship between representation, activism, and real-world change.

---

### **1. The Representation Paradox: Why More Visibility Isn’t Making Things Better**

The prevailing belief is that having more Muslims in politics, sports, media, and the arts should improve public perception and reduce prejudice. On the surface, the UK Muslim community appears to be a success story in this regard.

There are over 20 Muslim MPs in Parliament, a Muslim Mayor of London, and until recently, a Muslim First Minister of Scotland. The community is represented by Nadia Hussain, who famously baked a cake for the late queen; by world champion boxer Amir Khan; by social media influencers like Chunkz and rappers like Krept & Konan. Official Eid and Iftaar gatherings are held in Downing Street, Buckingham Palace, major cathedrals, and even Chelsea Football Club. **Muslim culture is undeniably present.**

The **shocking contradiction,** however, is that despite this unprecedented level of representation, key metrics show the situation for Muslims is actually getting worse. The undeniable fact is that rates of Islamophobic hate crime is higher than it has ever been.

We have more MPs, more mayors, more rappers, more sports people, more Muslims in every mainstream space. But data, statistics, and public opinion polls suggest that actually our situation is getting worse.

This paradox reveals a critical flaw in modern identity politics: visibility without power is a hollow victory. It forces a pivot from a strategy of assimilation and representation to one that demands structural influence and challenges the very systems that perpetuate prejudice.

---

### **2. The Great Awakening: The Generation Everyone Wrote Off**

Many our forefathers’ generation – defined by the events of 9/11 and the subsequent “War on Terror” – held a pessimistic view of the youth. They feared they had “lost” Gen Z to the distractions of social media, the complexities of culture wars, and a life of relative comfort that lacked a defining struggle.

The catalyst for a dramatic shift was the **genocide in Gaza**. It acted as a powerful and unexpected awakening, delivered directly through the one medium the older generation feared: social media. Unlike previous conflicts filtered by traditional news, this was a 4K live stream genocide – raw, immediate, and impossible to ignore. But it wasn’t just the visceral imagery that sparked action; it was the **profound political context**. This generation witnessed, in real-time, the complicity of the government of the country many of us call home and saw the horrific disparity between the dignity and the sanctity of the life of a Muslim Arab compared to a white Ukrainian.

This unfiltered witnessing of both violence and betrayal forced the younger generation to become a part of history. The result was an impressive wave of **organisation** and **mobilisation** that reinstated hope among their elders.

This awakening demonstrates how a generation fluent in digital media can transform online witnessing into powerful, real-world action. It shows that when confronted with undeniable injustice and hypocrisy, they can upend all expectations and claim their place on the front lines of history.

---

### **3. The Real Purpose of University: It’s Not Just About the Degree**

For most, university is a transactional step: you study hard, get a qualification, and secure a stable career. It is a means to an end.

However there is an urge from the Ummah to students to see their time on campus as something far more profound. University is a unique arena to discover new thoughts and opinions. It is a rare period in life with **the freedom to test ideas**, develop leadership skills, and build a resilient community – opportunities that may not be available again once professional and family life begins. But this training isn’t just for self-improvement; it comes with specific responsibilities. Students undeniably have a duty to convey the message of Islam to non-Muslims and to remove incorrect information and stereotypes which feed into the media’s distortion of Islam. Crucially, they also have a duty of care towards their fellow Muslim students who aren’t engaged… the ‘chillers’, the party goers, the drug dealers… because community strength is built from within.

University is a great opportunity for a student to view their time on campus not merely as academic preparation, but as a critical period for fulfilling a communal mission that can define their future impact on the world.

---

### **4. The 3 Non-Negotiable Ingredients for Change**

Drawing lessons from the young leaders of monumental, youth-led movements in Bangladesh, Syria, and Afghanistan, there are **three core principles** for anyone serious about making a difference.

- **Perseverance:** This is the understanding that victory is not instant and results are from a higher power. Rejecting the modern desire for an “Amazon Prime-style” delivery of social justice, it requires long-term vision. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ during the Battle of the Trenches, while surrounded and on the brink of defeat, gave his followers the “glad tidings of Rome and Persia” – a vision that seemed impossible at the time but anchored their struggle in a greater purpose.

- **Sacrifice:** Change demands a willingness to give up personal comfort for a cause. The speaker put this into stark perspective: While the ask to UK students is to sacrifice their “social time,” the young changemakers in other countries such as Bangladesh, Syria and Afghanistan sacrificed their comfort with their wealth, with their academic aspirations.” True success is not deserved without meaningful sacrifice.*“Say, “Surely my prayer, my sacrifice, my life, and my death are all for Allah – Lord of all worlds.”” [Surah Al Anam, Verse 162]*

- **Steadfastness:** This is the deep, unwavering belief that the cause is just and that liberation is a matter of “when, not if.” It is the conviction that the mission is greater than oneself, providing the resilience to endure setbacks.

This framework moves beyond simple “awareness-raising.” These three ingredients form a practical and disciplined code for any aspiring changemaker, grounding idealistic passion in the real-world commitment required to make a tangible impact.

---

### Conclusion: From Spectator to Change-maker

Tying these truths together, a clear message emerges: progress isn’t achieved through passive representation or by simply complaining about injustice. It is the product of active, resilient, and strategic engagement. It requires questioning assumptions, seizing opportunities, and cultivating the discipline to persevere through hardship.

The journey from being a spectator of history to becoming a shaper of it begins with a choice. As the Dilly Hussain’s words suggest, the most important question is the one you ask yourself. If history is shaped by those who answer the call, what will you build in the arena you’re in right now?
